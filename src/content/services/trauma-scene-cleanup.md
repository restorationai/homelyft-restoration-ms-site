## What Trauma Scene Cleanup actually involves

Trauma scene cleanup covers the removal and decontamination of biological material following a death, accident, or violent incident. This is distinct from general cleaning: it requires EPA-registered disinfectants capable of addressing bloodborne pathogens, controlled removal of contaminated materials like carpet, padding, or sections of drywall, and proper packaging and transport of regulated waste. Technicians work in full PPE and typically coordinate scene access with family members, property managers, or law enforcement, since many scenes aren't released for cleaning until an investigation or coroner's process concludes. Jobs range from a few hours for a contained, limited-exposure area to multiple days when flooring, subfloor, or structural materials have absorbed fluids over time.

## Our process

1. **Scene assessment and access coordination.** We confirm the scene has been released for cleaning, review access logistics with the family or property contact, and identify every surface or material that may be affected, not just what's immediately visible.
2. **PPE and containment staging.** Technicians suit up in protective equipment appropriate for bloodborne pathogen exposure, and we isolate the work area to prevent cross-contamination into unaffected parts of the home.
3. **Removal of contaminated materials.** Porous materials that have absorbed fluid, such as carpet, padding, or affected drywall, are removed rather than treated, since disinfecting a saturated porous surface doesn't reliably neutralize what's inside it.
4. **Decontamination and disinfection.** Remaining surfaces are cleaned with hospital-grade, EPA-registered disinfectants, following protocols for bloodborne pathogen remediation, with attention to seams, baseboards, and subfloor where fluid can travel.
5. **Waste handling and final documentation.** Regulated waste is packaged and transported in accordance with state and federal requirements, and we document the scope of work and materials removed for the family's records and any insurance claim.

## What separates a good trauma scene cleanup from a bad one

The most common failure in this work is treating it like a deep clean instead of a decontamination process. Surface disinfecting without removing saturated porous materials leaves pathogens and odor sources behind, which often surfaces weeks later as a persistent smell or a visible stain reappearing through paint. Another mistake is underestimating how far fluid travels beneath flooring or into wall cavities, missing contamination that later requires a second, more invasive cleanup. A thorough job documents every removed material with photos and a written scope, both for the family's peace of mind and because insurance carriers and property managers typically ask for this level of detail before approving coverage or re-occupancy.
