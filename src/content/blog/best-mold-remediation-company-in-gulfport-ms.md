## What actually separates a good mold remediation job from a bad one?

The difference comes down to five things: certification to a real standard, fixing the moisture source, proper containment, clearance testing, and whether the company can handle the rebuild. Skip any one of these and the mold is likely to come back within a season.

Ask specifically whether the crew is trained to the [IICRC S520 standard](https://iicrc.org/s520/), which requires source removal, containment to stop cross-contamination, and negative air pressure during demolition of affected materials. A company that just sprays bleach or an enzyme cleaner on visible growth without addressing the moisture source, per the [EPA's guidance on mold and moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), is treating a symptom, not the cause.

Clearance testing (a post-remediation air or surface sample, usually run by a third-party hygienist) confirms the space is back within normal fungal ecology before you move back in or reopen a commercial space. If the estimate doesn't mention clearance testing at all, that's worth asking about directly.

## Why does Gulf Coast humidity matter for mold remediation?

Because removing the mold without controlling the humidity that let it grow usually means it returns within months. Coastal Mississippi air carries enough ambient moisture that a remediation plan without dehumidification is incomplete from day one.

A thorough Gulfport job includes commercial dehumidifiers sized to the space, not just fans, running through the containment period and often for several days after. If the underlying cause was a slow roof leak, a failed flashing detail, or standing moisture under a pier-and-beam foundation common in older Gulfport and coastal neighborhoods, that source has to be repaired as part of the scope, not left for the homeowner to handle separately. ## What questions should I ask before hiring a mold remediation company?

Ask whether the technicians hold IICRC AMRT certification, whether the estimate includes a written scope with containment and clearance testing, and whether the company can also address the moisture source itself. A vague verbal quote with no containment plan is a warning sign.

Ask directly: Will you test the air or surfaces before and after the work? Who identifies and repairs the water intrusion, you or a separate contractor I have to hire? Is the containment plastic and negative air machinery part of this quote or billed separately later? A company that answers these clearly, in writing, before work starts is the one doing this correctly.
