If storm surge or rising water from a tropical system got into your Gulfport home, the cleanup bill looks different than a burst pipe. Outside floodwater typically runs $3 to $8 per square foot to clean up once flood cuts, extraction, and disinfection are included, with total jobs ranging from about $1,500 for a flooded crawl space to $40,000 or more for a whole-house loss with contents pack-out. The biggest swing factor isn't the square footage, it's whether the water came from a clean source or from outside.

## Why Does Outside Floodwater Cost More Than a Pipe Leak?

Outside floodwater, whether from storm surge, a rising bayou, or surface water running across the yard, is classified as Category 3 black water under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), and Category 3 cleanup costs more because contaminated porous materials have to come out instead of just drying in place.

A burst supply line starts as Category 1, clean water. It can often be extracted and dried without removing drywall or insulation. Floodwater that pushed in from the Mississippi Sound, a ditch, or a drainage canal carries silt, bacteria, fuel residue, and sometimes sewage. Once that water touches drywall, insulation, carpet pad, or particleboard cabinetry, those materials are treated as contaminated and removed, not dried. That's the line item that separates a $2,000 water call from a $10,000 flood call.

## What's Actually Included in the Price?

The bill breaks into four line items: extraction, flood cuts, disinfection, and structural drying, and each one adds cost because Category 3 water demands more than a wet-vac and a few fans.

**Extraction** comes first, usually with truck-mounted or portable pumps pulling standing water out before any demolition starts. **Flood cuts** are the next cost driver: technicians cut drywall and remove wet insulation at a set height above the visible water line, typically 12 to 24 inches above where the water reached, because wicking pulls contaminated moisture higher than it looks. That material gets bagged and disposed of, not dried.

**Disinfection and antimicrobial treatment** follow the demolition. Because Category 3 water carries bacteria and sometimes raw sewage, every surface the water touched gets treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial before drying equipment goes in. Skipping this step is why some DIY flood cleanups end up with a mold problem a few weeks later; the [EPA notes mold can start growing within 24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) on wet materials, and Category 3 jobs that sit untreated are at higher risk.

**Structural drying** closes out the job: air movers and LGR dehumidifiers run until moisture meter readings confirm the studs, subfloor, and remaining drywall are back to dry-standard, which is checked daily, not guessed at.

## Does Insurance Cover Flood Damage, or Do I Need a Separate Policy?

A standard homeowners policy does not cover flood damage from storm surge, rising water, or surface water flowing in from outside; that requires a separate flood policy through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood carrier. This catches a lot of Gulf Coast homeowners off guard, because the same policy that pays for a burst pipe inside the house usually excludes water that came in from outside, no matter how the storm caused it.

If you carry an NFIP policy, [FloodSmart.gov](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) walks through how to start a claim and what documentation the adjuster needs, generally photos of the water line, a room-by-room inventory of damaged contents, and receipts for any emergency mitigation. Homeowners without flood coverage are paying for flood cleanup out of pocket or through FEMA individual assistance when it's declared, which is a different and more limited program than an NFIP payout. ## What Drives the Cost Up: Raised Homes, Slab Homes, and Crawl Spaces

How a Gulfport home is built changes where the water goes, and that changes the bill. A slab-on-grade home with floodwater inside usually means flood cuts and flooring replacement across every affected room, since there's no crawl space to catch the water below floor level.

A raised home, common closer to the Mississippi Sound and the bayou-adjacent streets around Gulfport, often takes water into the crawl space or lower-level utility area even when the main living floor stayed dry. That still requires extraction, insulation removal from the underside of the subfloor, and antimicrobial treatment in a tight, low-clearance space, which takes longer per square foot than open-room work. Homes with an encapsulated or vented crawl space sometimes see less insulation damage, but standing water under the house for more than a day or two still means pulling wet batt insulation and checking the subfloor for soft spots before drying begins.

## What Happens During a Flood Cleanup Call?

1. **Safety check and water categorization.** Crews confirm the power is safe to work around and document the water's source before anything else.
2. **Extraction.** Standing water comes out first, usually within the first hour or two on site.
3. **Flood cuts and material removal.** Contaminated drywall, insulation, and flooring are cut out and bagged for disposal.
4. **Disinfection.** Affected surfaces get an antimicrobial treatment before drying equipment goes in.
5. **Structural drying with daily monitoring.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture readings hit dry standard.
6. **Documentation.** Photos, moisture logs, and a written scope are compiled for the insurance claim or NFIP file.
