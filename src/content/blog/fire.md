## What Should You Do in the First 24 Hours After a Fire?

In the first 24 hours after a house fire, call your insurance company, keep the power off until the utility or an electrician clears it, and board up broken windows or doors before leaving the property unattended overnight.

Do these in order:

- **Report the claim same-day.** Most policies require prompt notice, and an early claim number speeds up every step after it.
- **Photograph everything before you touch anything.** Char patterns, water pooling from suppression efforts, and soot streaks all document the loss for the adjuster.
- **Don't run the HVAC system.** A running air handler pulls soot particles through the ductwork and spreads them to rooms the fire never reached.
- **Don't wipe soot with water or household cleaners.** Wet soot smears and bonds to painted surfaces, turning a cleanable stain into a replacement job.
- **Get the structure boarded up or tarped** if windows, a roof section, or an exterior wall are open to weather.

## What Does the Fire Damage Restoration Process Involve?

Fire damage restoration moves through four phases: emergency mitigation, soot and odor removal, structural drying if firefighting water was used, and reconstruction of damaged framing, drywall, and flooring.

Mitigation happens first, usually within hours of the fire being extinguished: board-up, tarping, and HEPA air scrubbers to stop soot from migrating further through the house. Soot and odor removal follows, cleaning surfaces to the [IICRC](https://iicrc.org) S700 fire restoration standard, which sets the method and documentation for how soot is tested and removed by material type. If firefighters used water on the fire, structural drying runs alongside the soot work using the same equipment and documentation standard as a water loss. Reconstruction, the last phase, rebuilds whatever had to come out: charred framing, water-damaged subfloor, drywall, cabinetry. ## Will Homeowners Insurance Cover Fire Damage Restoration?

Most Mississippi homeowners policies cover fire damage, including smoke and soot cleanup, under the dwelling and personal property sections, but what you actually collect depends on the cause of the fire and whether your policy pays replacement cost or actual cash value.

Actual cash value policies subtract depreciation from what it costs to replace a damaged item or finish; replacement cost policies pay the full cost with no depreciation deducted, though often in two payments. Check your declarations page for which one you carry. Most policies also include Additional Living Expenses (ALE) coverage, which reimburses hotel stays and meals while the home is unlivable, usually for a set number of months or a dollar cap. Keep every receipt. For contents, a documented inventory, photos, and an itemized list of what's salvageable versus a total loss protects the claim far better than a verbal description to the adjuster.
